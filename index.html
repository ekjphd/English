English B.A. track

Creative Writing

The creative writing program introduces students to fiction, poetry, and nonfiction prose. Students study published work and produce their own, learning the craft through the study of theory and the close analysis of their own writing.

Creative writing students choose a genre to specialize in: fiction, poetry, scriptwriting, or creative nonfiction. They advance from Level I to Level II workshops, where they share original work and respond to the work of their peers.

Core courses

CRW 3013Creative Writing for English Majors

An introduction to the literary genres. Students study the foundational elements of craft through critical reading of established authors and practice critiquing the writing of their peers.

CRW 3053Theory & Practice of Creative Writing

An examination of the genres and readings of contemporary creative writing. Students read published texts closely to understand literary technique and discuss current issues in the field.

ENG 3014Theories and Techniques of Literature Study

A gateway course in contemporary approaches to the academic study of literature, covering techniques of analysis and theories of interpretation. It is the prerequisite for all 4000-level literature courses.

Genre workshops

Students take at least two Level I and two Level II workshops.

CRW 3120 / 4122Fiction

Level I focuses on short fiction. Students read and discuss published stories, workshop their own, and write feedback on classmates' work. Level II offers intensive writing practice and peer critique of original manuscripts.

CRW 3310 / 4320Poetry

Level I is for students who want to read, write, and experiment with contemporary poetry, with group analysis and close reading of poetic theory. Level II deepens the study of contemporary poetry alongside intensive work on original manuscripts.

CRW 3610 / 4616Scriptwriting

Level I covers the theory and practice of writing scripts for film and television. Level II is an advanced workshop with intensive writing projects and peer critique of original scripts.

CRW 3211 / 4224Creative Nonfiction

Level I is for students who want to turn lived experience into compelling writing, with class response to original work and readings in the genre. Level II covers memoir, personal essay, and literary journalism, drawing on memory, observation, and research.

English B.A. track

Literature

The literature program helps students develop the ability to read discerningly, think critically, and write clearly. Studying literature in its historical, cultural, and theoretical contexts reveals the ways of people and society, while encouraging the lifelong pleasure to be found in this art form.

Students read across centuries and traditions, from Chaucer and Shakespeare to Native American, Caribbean, and Latinx literature, and complete an in-depth study of a single major author.

English B.A. track

Technical Communication

Technical communication prepares students to meet the communication needs of the business and scientific community. Students learn to apply the principles of written, spoken, and visual communication to a wide variety of situations, using current software and hardware in the department's Technical Writing Laboratory.

The track builds toward professional work: students develop a book-length documentation project, then collaborate with an industry or nonprofit client to produce a final published product.

Digital Humanities in the Age of AI

The Digital Humanities in the Age of AI minor invites students to explore technologies that shape culture, including generative and agentic AI. Students use these tools to solve humanities problems and work with code, text, and images to build humanities projects.

The minor combines two approaches: humanistic ways of examining digital texts, from social media to video games to chatbots, and digital methods for analyzing humanistic texts, such as data mining the language of novels or digitally archiving history.

Linguistics is the scientific study of language at every level, from sounds and gestures to words, sentences, meaning, and how language changes over time.

The interdisciplinary minor suits students in English, modern languages, anthropology, computer science, legal studies, psychology, communication, and anyone interested in how language works.

Featured Courses

A selection of courses on the department's 2026–2027 schedule. Select a course to read more.

ENL 3451, Spring 2027Tolkien's BookshelfThe poetry and legends that shaped Middle-earth.

A section of Topics in British Literature that studies the older poetry and legends Tolkien drew on to build Middle-earth, starting from moments such as the lament Aragorn chants as he approaches Théoden's hall. It counts as pre-1865 literature.

LIT 3482, Spring 2027Zombies: Undead Media from the Gothic to the AlgorithmThe zombie in literature, film, and games.

A section of Literature & Popular Culture that traces the undead from Haitian Vodou narratives and Gothic fiction to graphic novels and post-apocalyptic stories, asking how the zombie expresses real anxieties about systemic failure and public health.

CRW 4125, Spring 2027WorldbuildingBuild a world together, then write in it.

Students read fiction, essays, theory, and poetry about how writers craft immersive worlds. The class builds a world together, and each student writes a full-length project set in it. The course will likely include a visit to the university archives.

CRW 3713, Fall 2026Writing for Video GamesDesign a game and write its story.

An introduction to game design, including brainstorming, gameplay, artificial intelligence, and storytelling in interactive systems. Students conceive and outline their own game and write interactive scripts for cut scenes and scripted events, which are workshopped in class.

ENG 3861, Spring 2027Digital Humanities Approaches to AIHow AI is changing culture and creativity.

Students explore the history of artificial intelligence and its effect on culture and creativity. They practice using AI tools in digital humanities projects, learn the opportunities and limits of these tools, and document their own use of AI.

LIN 4660, Spring 2027Linguistics and LiteratureResearch for the Oxford English Dictionary.

Students research earlier uses of words for the Oxford English Dictionary, then join the team preparing Noah Webster's Dictionary Online, an NEH-funded project. Work includes archival research, proofreading, XML markup, image editing, and beta testing.

LIT 3931, Summer 2026Viking Literature: Myths, Legends, and SagasThor, Odin, Loki, and the Icelandic sagas.

An introduction to Old Norse literature: myths of Thor, Odin, and Loki, tales of legendary heroes and cursed swords, and the sagas of the Icelanders, which may be the earliest examples of historical fiction.

ENL 4303, Spring 2027British Authors: Jane AustenAll six of Austen's published novels.

Students read Northanger Abbey, Sense and Sensibility, Pride and Prejudice, Mansfield Park, Emma, and Persuasion, along with a short biography of Austen. The Fall 2026 section of British Authors focuses on the Brontë sisters.

Honors Undergraduate ThesisUCF's oldest undergraduate research program, established in 1989.

Students work one-on-one with a faculty member, design an independent reading project, take a graduate seminar or workshop, and write a critical, creative, or technical thesis. The thesis is defended and published online through the UCF Libraries.

To apply, students need 12 upper-division credit hours at UCF, an overall GPA of at least 3.4, an English GPA of at least 3.5, and a faculty member willing to serve as thesis advisor.
